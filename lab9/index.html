Lab 9: Geospatial Visualization

Two coordinated views of one variable - 2025 nominal GDP for the world's 50 largest economies. In the first, color carries the value (a choropleth); in the second, area carries it (a cartogram). Hovering a country in either view previews it in both; clicking pins the selection so it stays highlighted, and clicking it again clears it.

1. Dataset

The GDP figures are the 50 largest economies by 2025 nominal GDP (current prices, billions of U.S. dollars) from the IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2025. Some observations are IMF staff estimates and may later be revised. Country boundaries come from a world GeoJSON in which every feature carries an ISO-3 code; that code is the key used to join the two files. Economies outside the top 50 are shown as missing data, not as zero.

2. 2025 GDP Choropleth

Color encodes 2025 GDP: darker means larger. Hover a country for its exact value and rank, scroll to zoom, and drag to pan.

Grey countries are outside the top-50 dataset (missing data, not zero). The legend and its tick spacing update with the selected scale.

Color scale choice. 2025 GDP is heavily skewed: the United States (30,616 B) and China (19,399 B) dwarf a long tail of economies under 1,000 B. On a linear scale almost every country collapses into the palest shade while only two go dark, so the map cannot separate the mid-tier. A logarithmic scale spreads those economies across distinct shades and is the default here; a square-root scale is a milder compromise. Use the selector to compare - the logarithmic scale gives the most readable map for this distribution.

3. 2025 GDP Cartogram

Each economy is a circle whose area is proportional to its 2025 GDP (radius scaled by the square root of GDP). Position is an approximate geographic layout; circles are pushed apart so none overlap. Hover any circle for its exact value and rank; the largest economies are labelled with their ISO-3 code.

Area encodes GDP. True shape and exact position are deliberately sacrificed so that magnitude and rank can be read directly.

4. Design and Comparison

On the choropleth the United States and China read as the darkest regions, but because a few very large economies compress the scale, most of the top 50 look similarly pale and are hard to separate. On the cartogram the United States dominates as by far the largest circle, with China second and Japan, Germany, India and the United Kingdom forming a clear next tier. Countries with large territory but moderate output, such as Russia, Canada, Australia and Brazil, shrink sharply once area encodes GDP rather than land; small, wealthy economies such as Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands and Switzerland grow relative to their tiny footprints. The choropleth preserves real position, borders and adjacency, so it answers geographic questions: which regions are wealthy, how neighbours compare, where the gaps sit. The cartogram discards true shape and area and keeps only approximate position, but it makes magnitude and rank immediate, because the eye compares area far more accurately than shade. The choropleth is therefore best for "where" questions and regional pattern; the cartogram is best for "how much" and ranking. Moving from color to area changes the sense of scale: the concentration of world output in a handful of economies, easy to miss among near-identical shades, becomes unmistakable when the United States circle towers over every other.
